## The Afternoon That Reset the Benchmark

The previous record stood at 8,656 MW from June 2024, and the 2025 peak reached 8,442 MW. This year’s figure sits about 3.6% above 2025, and Delhi crossed the 8,000 MW line three times in June alone. A city-wide rise of that size looks modest on paper.

Inside a single building the swing is usually sharper, because new load arrives in lumps: a chiller added in phase two, a fitted-out floor, a row of fast chargers in the parking level. A transformer that was comfortable at commissioning can be running hot within two summers.

## Ambient Heat Eats Nameplate Capacity

Standard transformer ratings assume a 40°C ambient. Delhi’s afternoons in May and June regularly exceed that, and a closed substation room adds its own heat on top. Insulation ageing follows a harsh rule: the rate roughly doubles for every 6°C of extra hot-spot temperature, as IEC 60076–7 describes. A winding running 8°C hotter than design therefore ages about two and a half times faster. A transformer can show a healthy load meter and still be spending its life far too quickly.

## Load Profile Beats Connected Load

Take an office block with 1,800 kW connected load. Apply a diversity of 0.65 and the demand falls to 1,170 kW. At 0.95 power factor that is about 1,230 kVA, and a 20% growth margin lifts the figure to nearly 1,480 kVA. The arithmetic is simple, but it only holds when the diversity figure is honest. Cooling plant tends to peak together in the late afternoon, so assuming the chillers will politely take turns is how undersized transformers are born.

Three items deserve real numbers before a rating is frozen:

- Starting method and inrush current of chillers and VRF compressors
- Harmonic content from UPS systems, drives and LED drivers
- Planned EV charging load and rooftop solar export

Harmonics deserve special mention. Non-linear loads raise eddy-current losses, so a standard transformer delivers less usable capacity than its plate suggests. Either derate it or specify a K-rated unit.

Power factor correction needs the same care: plain capacitor banks can resonate with harmonic currents and overheat, so detuned reactors belong in the panel from day one. Tap settings matter too. Summer voltage at the far end of a feeder often sags, and a transformer left on its winter tap forces the building’s motors to draw more current for the same work.

## Redundancy Before Rating

One large transformer is the cheapest line item and the weakest design. Two units, each carrying around 60% of the normal load, run cooler and keep the building alive when one is taken out for maintenance or trips. A bus coupler and a clear load-shedding order, with cooling for critical areas kept alive and decorative lighting shed first, turn that spare capacity into real resilience.

Dry-type cast resin units suit basements and high-rise cores, since they avoid oil fire risk and need tighter clearances. Oil-filled units cost less, tolerate short overloads better and belong in outdoor yards with proper plinths, soak pits and fire separation. Delhi’s approval authorities look hard at this choice, and the drawings should show it clearly.

## The Room Is Part of the Transformer

A 1,000 kVA unit sheds roughly 10 kW of heat at full load, which is the equivalent of several room air conditioners running inside a concrete box. Louvres sized for that heat, exhaust fans on temperature control, and clear air paths around the enclosure are as important as the winding itself. Fan-assisted cooling stages can add around a quarter to the rating, but only when the fans are maintained, and the room can actually shed the extra heat.

## Planning for the Next Record

Rooftop solar reduces grid import at midday yet can push power back toward the transformer, so protection settings need review. EV chargers pile on evening load while the unit is still shedding the day’s heat. Neither shows up in a decade-old load schedule. A sizing exercise worth trusting is revisited whenever a major load is added, not filed away after energisation.

Winding and oil temperature indicators wired into the building management system give the trend data that makes this possible. Three summers of logged readings tell a facility team more than any nameplate, and they show when a spare bay or a second unit should be funded, long before an alarm does.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q1. Should a transformer be loaded to 100% of its rating in Delhi summers?

No. Sustained loading near full rating in 45°C-plus ambient shortens insulation life quickly. Most designers keep normal peak loading around 60–70% of rating, leaving headroom for heat, harmonics, and growth. The exact figure depends on cooling class, room ventilation, and the building’s load profile.

### Q2. Is a dry-type transformer better than oil-filled for basements?

Usually yes for indoor and basement locations, because cast resin units avoid oil fire risk and need smaller clearances. Oil-filled units cost less and handle overloads better, so they suit outdoor yards. The choice should follow fire norms, available space, and the approval requirements for the building.

### Q3. Do harmonics reduce usable transformer capacity?

Yes. Harmonic currents from UPS units, drives, and LED drivers raise eddy-current losses and winding temperature, so a standard transformer delivers less usable kVA. Designers either derate the unit or specify a K-rated transformer, based on measured or estimated harmonic content at the supply point.

### Q4. Can two smaller transformers be safer than one large one?

Often, yes. Two units sharing the load give continuity when one fails or goes for maintenance, and each runs cooler at partial loading. The trade-off is higher cost and floor space, so the design must also include a bus coupler and clear load-shedding priorities.
